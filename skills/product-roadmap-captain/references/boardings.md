## The four boardings

Play each person plainly: their role, their question, no accent or costume. Ask the hardest question about this founder's roadmap, then up to two follow-ups that press on the weakest part of the answer. Ask in the founder's language.

| Who boards | Hardest question | What it exposes |
|---|---|---|
| The customer | What would we lose if you disappeared, and does your roadmap make that bigger? | Value that lives in the demo, not in use |
| The buyer | Can we justify your price, risk and lock-in over three years? | No answer on data rights, model change or exit |
| The regulator | Which of your initiatives need permissions you do not hold yet? | Compliance scheduled after launch |
| The investor | Why will this bet reach the target, and what will you prove before your next raise? | Initiatives that do not add up |

## Verdicts

End each boarding with one verdict and its reason in one sentence. It is the verdict of a rehearsal; say so, and in any document write it as `rehearsal verdict`:

- **Held:** answered with evidence: a labelled figure, a source, a date, an owner.
- **Honest:** no evidence, but the founder named the gap and what it becomes.
- **Weak:** an answer with nothing behind it, or a claim the roadmap contradicts.
- **Silent:** no answer.

For every question not answered with evidence, ask which it becomes: a risk on the roadmap, a change in sequence, or a cut. Record the choice.

## The six building blocks

For each block, ask the roadmap question first and the product question second. A product question without evidence becomes a stated risk, a dependency or an early initiative. Never a hidden assumption.

| Block | Roadmap question | Product question |
|---|---|---|
| Big statement | Can a chief executive or board member repeat it? | Is there a growing segment that pays today? |
| Today to target | Is it the same business metric at both ends? | Does today's paying base show the model can scale? |
| Growth initiatives | Three to five levers, each adding a number to the target? | Does the AI and data advantage carry into each new market? |
| Value and return | Value, cost, payback and return for every initiative? | Do unit economics hold after AI running costs? |
| Sequence | Enablers and compliance first? | Can the team serve ten times the customers without ten times the effort? |
| Signals to adapt | What would make the founder re-sequence or drop an initiative? | Are those signals measured today? |

## Disruptions

Run two. The standard pair:

- **A funding delay of six months.**
- **An assurance delay:** a certification, audit or permission arrives later than planned.

The founder may swap one for a market event from this list:

- An economic downturn: budgets across the segment freeze.
- The main customer is lost.
- A platform changes its terms: the model, cloud or app-store provider changes price, access or rules.
- A buyer withdraws its commitment.
- A procurement or tender is lost.
- A new competitor arrives.
- Know-how is lost: a key person leaves.

For each disruption ask: what becomes exposed, the one roadmap change that answers it, and the evidence needed to confirm the change.

Then, for each initiative: what it waits on, and who is short.

## Roots to name

When answers keep failing in the same way, name the root you see, once:

- **Tech-push:** building first, finding the buyer later.
- **The pilot catch-22,** a loop: no pilots, so no data, so no evaluation, so no trust, so no pilots. Usually the root most worth breaking first, because one well-designed pilot breaks the loop.
- **Thin capacity:** a roadmap sized for a team, carried by one or two people.
- **Feature roadmaps:** timelines of things to build with no gates between them.
